Per-post efficiency

Boosted contest reels averaged 47 follows per post; non-contest organic posts averaged 4.6 follows per post. The contest mechanic is roughly 10x more efficient at follower acquisition than regular posting, before paid amplification. Boosting then doubles that efficiency. The strategic implication: the biggest lever isn't the boost itself, it's the contest format. Run contests more often; boost the ones that matter most.

Recommendation

Continue and selectively scale.

The math works. Test a scaled spend on the Broken Social Scene episode — $50 over 5 days instead of $30 over 3 — to see if performance scales linearly or hits diminishing returns. Bigger band, broader fan base, worth the experiment.

Worth testing

Boosted spend on tactical reels.

Contest invites are the only post type currently boosted. Tactical reels (Bob Rock, Fat Wreck) drive the highest save rates organically and may benefit from a small ad-spend test — the demographic that saves these is the demographic sponsors care about. Try $20 over 2 days on the next one.

Note: Arkells contest received an additional small TikTok boost (~$15) which is excluded from the cost-per-follow calculation since it was experimental. The 46 follows attributed are Instagram-only.

Data gap: 30-day retention of contest-acquired followers is not in current exports. Pull from IG Insights → Audience → Followers timeline, segmented to the days following each contest reel. If retention is above 70 percent, scale aggressively. If below, the cheap CPF is illusory and the strategy needs a rethink.

Cross-promotion mechanics

Who gets onto the band's grid.

A manual audit of every post in the period, focused on cross-grid exposure: which posts ended up on the band's grid versus which stayed only on D2D's. Important framing: collab acceptances and band re-uploads function identically. Both put a post on the band's grid; both expose it to the band's followers. They are treated as one mechanic throughout.

Cross-grid posts per episode

Number of D2D posts about each episode that landed on the band's grid — via collab acceptance or fresh upload from the band's account. Both count equally.

Finding 01

Cross-grid exposure varies 9x across episodes.

From Reklaws (1 post on the band's grid across the entire episode cycle) to Limblifter via Jeremy Taggart (9 posts) — a nine-fold range. Drummers themselves accept at much higher rates than band-official accounts. Taggart accepted everything; Limblifter (the band account) accepted about half. Same pattern with Theodore McKibbon vs The Trews account, Adam Hindle vs Dwayne Gretzky.

Finding 02

Bands accept the headline post but decline supporting reels.

The pattern: most bands accept cross-grid exposure on the season anchor and one or two supporting reels, but decline contest reels, behind-the-scenes content, and thank-you posts. This is not a problem to solve — it reflects how band social teams allocate scarce grid space. The strategic move is to lock in the right posts, not all of them.

Finding 03

For boosted contests, cross-grid isn't needed.

The Trews stick contest reel — 103 follows, the highest follow conversion in the dataset — had zero cross-grid exposure. For boosted reels, ad targeting handles reach. Cross-grid is an organic-reach mechanic; the contest mechanic is paid amplification. Different levers, both work.

Finding 04

Hashtag count is decorative.

Posts with three hashtags and posts with eight perform within the same range. #drummertodrummer, #doubledrums, and #drummersofinstagram appear on most posts and are worth keeping. Beyond five tags, returns flatten. Caption length follows the same rule.

The actionable takeaway

Make cross-grid exposure part of the booking conversation. When you ask a drummer to come on the show, include the social ask in the same message: a request to share or accept collab tags on the launch post and one supporting reel. Reach out to band managers ahead of posting if you have their contact, but route through the drummer when possible — they accept at meaningfully higher rates than band-official social teams. Taggart hit 100 percent across nine posts; that is the model.

What this opens up for ad-spend testing

Right now contest invites are the only boosted type, averaging $0.64 per follow. Two more types are worth testing on a small budget: tactical reels (Bob Rock, Fat Wreck-style content with high save rates — sponsors care about saves) and episode trailers for high-recognition guests (where boost could amplify launch-day peak views). Skip teasers, winners, and 360 promos for paid spend — their organic performance suggests ad dollars would be wasted there.

Sponsor narrative

Four pillars for the media kit.

These four findings convert directly into a sponsor case. Each pillar answers a specific question drum or cymbal brands will ask before signing on.

01

A precisely targeted demographic.

80 percent men, 86 percent aged 25 to 54, 72 percent Canadian. The audience over-indexes by 34 percentage points on the male skew versus the Canadian Meta baseline. This is the exact demographic drum and cymbal brands target with their marketing dollars — D2D delivers it without the wastage of broader-interest programming.

02

Niche audience that converts cheaply.

1,481 Instagram followers (+100 percent since January) and 1,918 YouTube subscribers (+10.7 percent in period). Eight contest reels boosted at $30 each pulled 374 follows for $240 spend — a $0.64 average cost per follow, at the low end of the $0.50–$2.00 industry benchmark. The Trews stick contest converted 103 follows at $0.29 each, proof that smaller, more enthusiastic fan bases convert at higher rates than broader audiences.

03

Proven brand integration.

YC Drum Company spec post pulled 5,037 organic views, direct proof that drum-brand-specific content performs. Bob Rock production story drove 33 saves; Fat Wreck story drove 18. 97.58 percent positive sentiment on YouTube — brand-safe environment.

04

Cross-grid distribution multiplier.

When D2D content lands on a band's grid — whether via accepted collab tag or fresh upload from their account — reach multiplies. The Arkells trailer drove 38,694 cross-platform views across two coordinated uploads — 3,720 from D2D, plus 34,974 from @arkellsmusic (21,920 IG + 13,054 Facebook). Limblifter's Jeremy Taggart engaged with 9 posts on his grid across the episode cycle. The mechanic compounds across a season and an 11-band roster.

Merch launch

Shopify, smartly.

The Shopify Lite store is live with Instagram integration but hasn't been promoted yet. Below: a launch sequence calibrated to the audience, the platform, and the show's tone.

Audience read

The buyer is already in the data.

80 percent men, 86 percent aged 25–54, 72 percent Canadian, Toronto-Hamilton-Halifax concentration. Established budget for niche music gear and clear emotional engagement with the show via contest entries. Prioritize Canadian and US fulfillment; the international tail is small enough to deprioritize at launch.

Platform read

Instagram is the only store-relevant channel.

Shopify Lite's IG integration unlocks shoppable posts, product tags in Reels, and an in-app Shop tab. Enable all three before any promotional content goes live, and back-tag products into 3–5 existing posts where merch is already visible. YouTube descriptions and Spotify show notes should also link the store URL.

01

Hero drop carousel.

A single, focused launch post: Adam wearing the shirt, close-up shots of the print, and a simple caption. One post, one product, one clear ask. Don't bury the launch in a multi-product announcement — the IG algorithm rewards focused intent.

02

Discount code for past contest entrants.

Hundreds of people enter each contest; only one wins. DM every non-winning entrant a small discount code (e.g., 15 percent off) once the winner is announced. Converts losers into customers, leverages the contest CRM you've already built, and costs nothing to run. Free dollar driver attached to a system already in motion.

03

Shirt the drummer guests.

Give every drummer guest a free shirt at the end of the interview. Some will wear it for the closing pose-with-Adam shot — that photo lives in the BTS / thank-you carousel and lands in front of their followers. Free distribution to the highest-leverage influencer in each episode. Some shirts get worn beyond the shoot; that's a bonus.

04

Adam wears the shirt.

Adam wearing the merch during interviews and double-drum sessions turns every reel and trailer into passive product placement. Legitimately tag the product in the post (the IG Shop integration recognizes products on-camera) and the algorithm treats every clip as a shoppable surface. No separate product post needed; the show's existing content does the work.

Pitfalls to avoid

Don't hard-sell — the audience is here for the show, not the storefront, and a heavy push will alienate them. Don't launch with too many SKUs at once; one or two hero items first, expand based on data. Don't ignore product tagging in Reels — that's where the most-engaged audience lives, and a Reel without a product tag is a missed sale. Don't skip the IG Shop tab on the profile — it's free real estate that signals the store exists.

Next moves

Four concrete actions.

These moves are sequenced for impact. The first two are repeatable for every episode going forward. Moves three and four set up the sponsor pitch directly — Broken Social Scene is the upcoming inflection point and the right episode to apply every learning at full strength.

Move 01

Build cross-grid exposure into the booking conversation.

When you ask a drummer to come on the show, include the social ask in the same message: “happy to feature you, and we'd ask that you share or accept collab tags on the launch post and one supporting reel.” If you have band manager contact, loop them in ahead of posting. Route through the drummer when possible — acceptance rates are meaningfully higher than band-official accounts. Taggart hit 100 percent over 9 posts.

Move 02

Pre-confirm collab acceptance before publish.

When inviting collabs, send a quick check-in to the drummer's social manager (or the drummer directly) before the post goes live. About one in three invitations currently go unaccepted — a pre-publish ping closes that gap.

Move 03

Test scaled contest spend on Broken Social Scene.

Contest CPF averages $0.64. For BSS, try $50 over 5 days instead of $30 over 3 to see if performance scales. Pull 30-day retention on Trews and Taggart contest cohorts first to validate that contest follows actually stick.

Move 04

Test small ad spend on tactical reels.

Bob Rock and Fat Wreck reels saved at high rates organically. Try $20 over 2 days on the next tactical reel to see if paid amplification compounds the save behavior. Same audience targeting as contest invites; very different conversion mechanic.
